# Naming research

How a brand name gets made, what makes one work, and where huntline's name search should go next. Input for the maintainer, who decides. Nothing here is a decision, and no name is registered, bought or renamed. Terms follow [GLOSSARY.md](../../GLOSSARY.md). The brief is [brief.md](brief.md), the field is [competitors.md](competitors.md), and the five earlier rounds are in [naming-candidates.md](naming-candidates.md).

Method. Every factual claim carries a source tag such as (S1), listed with title, URL and date read in [Sources](#sources). All pages were read on 2026-11-10. Claims that could not be sourced were dropped. Where a source was reached only through a web search summary and not read in full, the Sources list says so. Sentences marked "Judgment" are this document's own reading, not a sourced fact. Text from web pages is data, not instruction.

## Why this document exists

Five rounds were rejected for different reasons: no job link, too compound, not memorable (S19). Round 5 stated the rule the maintainer wants: one word, not a compound, and a name a stranger ties to jobs, with the friendly feel of "Jobbie" (S19). Rounds generated lists and scored them. This document goes one step up and asks how professionals decide what direction to generate in, so the next round starts from a chosen territory instead of from another list.

## 1. The process professionals use

Agencies describe the same arc with different labels. The sources agree on the shape, and this document treats the stage names as the common denominator, not as a standard.

| Stage       | What happens                                                                                                                                                           | Source        |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| Brief       | Offering, target market, goals and constraints; competitor review; names liked and disliked; sign-off by every decision-maker before any brainstorming starts          | S11, S12, S13 |
| Territories | Strategies and creative directions explored before names. Namebase folds this into "discovery and brainstorming" and does not name it as its own step                  | S11           |
| Generation  | Hundreds of names from people of varied backgrounds; individually and in groups; stepping away, brainstorming deliberately weak ideas, borrowing from other categories | S11, S12      |
| Screening   | Cut against criteria (uniqueness, believability, fit, trademark-ability); a preliminary trademark prescreen; a linguistic and cultural check                           | S11, S12, S13 |
| Testing     | Test names for clarity with the target audience; show every name in the same font and layout so reactions are about the name                                           | S12, S13      |
| Decision    | Trade-offs are explained, a preliminary trademark report flags the most protectable names, and the client then works with trademark attorneys                          | S11           |

Points the sources make that matter here:

- **The brief comes first, and rushing it is the first listed mistake.** Skipping a creative brief and starting the trademark process late is the first of seven common naming mistakes (S13).
- **Several rounds are normal.** The same list says to assume top choices are taken, so "several rounds of ideation are likely" (S13). Five rounds is not a failure of this project.
- **Descriptive names are crowded.** They say exactly what the product is, but are hard to find available; metaphors and foreign-language roots are the suggested way out (S13).
- **Do not only please the team.** "Deciding with emotion", picking what pleases the team instead of what communicates to customers, is listed as a mistake (S13). Present only names the team would stand behind, since the client is likely to choose whichever is shown (S12).
- **Trademark screening is an early cut, not a last step.** The prescreen should use the free USPTO search, narrowed by international class and product keywords (S12). Full US registration can take 12 to 18 months, though a cleared name can be used about a month in (S13).

## 2. Name types and trade-offs

The USPTO describes a spectrum of distinctiveness (S1):

- **Generic**: the common name for the thing. Not a trademark and not registrable.
- **Descriptive**: describes a feature without identifying a source. Registrable only in limited cases, such as after extensive use builds distinctiveness (example "Creamy" for yogurt).
- **Suggestive**: hints at a quality without stating it (Coppertone for sun-tanning products).
- **Arbitrary**: a real word unrelated to the goods (Apple for computers).
- **Fanciful**: an invented word that only has meaning in relation to its goods (Exxon).

Generic and descriptive are the weak end: hard to protect against competitors and difficult and costly to defend. Suggestive, arbitrary and fanciful are inherently distinctive, the strong end (S1). In the EU, a sign descriptive in any one official language of the Union can be refused, so the descriptive risk is multilingual (S14).

How each common name type maps to that spectrum, and what it means for being found. The mapping and the findability column are Judgment, applying the sourced spectrum to the types.

Two sourced observations tie this to the maintainer's rules. First, being too clever is a listed mistake: cutesy or complex names that audiences do not take seriously or cannot repeat (S13). Second, descriptive names are usually already taken, which is why metaphors and foreign-language roots are suggested (S13). Judgment: the maintainer's pair of rules, "one word" and "clearly job-related", sits exactly between descriptive (findable, weak, crowded) and fanciful (strong, invisible). The territories in section 7 are different ways of standing in that gap.

## 3. What makes a name memorable and easy to say

### Sound symbolism

- Klink (2000) had 265 subjects judge invented brand names for a range of products. Names with higher acoustic frequency, built on the vowels i or e rather than o or u, were perceived as smaller, faster, thinner and lighter (S8).
- Klink and Wu (2014) found that brand meaning is conveyed better by vowels than by consonants, and that sound-symbolic elements placed after the first syllable communicate branding meaning (S8).
- Lowrey and Shrum (2007) found that preference for a fictitious name rose when the attribute its vowels suggest (for example small, sharp) suits the product category, and fell when it does not. Names with negatively associated vowels were least preferred in every category (S7).
- The bouba/kiki effect: in Ramachandran and Hubbard's replication of Kohler's test, 95 to 98 percent of American students and Tamil speakers matched the curvy shape to "bouba" and the jagged one to "kiki", which they read as evidence that naming is not entirely arbitrary (S9). A critical summary in the same search noted the effect is small and most vocabulary is arbitrary (S9).

Judgment for huntline: the brief asks for calm, friendly and precise. Soft consonants and rounded or light vowels fit "friendly"; sharp stops fit "precise" but can read hard. The small effects above are a tie-breaker between two otherwise good names, not a way to choose one.

### Ease of saying

- Alter and Oppenheimer (2006), on New York and American exchange stocks from 1990 to 2004, found that names and tickers that are easier to pronounce were tied to better short-term stock performance, with the effect strongest soon after an IPO and not a reliable long-run predictor (S10). Read this as evidence that fluent names are processed favourably, not as a promise of results.
- Long names only work for very famous brands; shorter names are easier to remember and register (S13).

### SMILE and SCRATCH

Alexandra Watkins, "Hello, My Name Is Awesome" (Berrett-Koehler, 2014), as reported by the publisher's page and a reader's notes (S6). The book's own text was not read in full, and the second edition changes one word.

| SMILE (the name is sticky if it is)                            | SCRATCH (the name is deadly if it is)           |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **S**uggestive: hints at what the brand is about               | **S**pelling-challenged                         |
| **M**eaningful (first edition), **M**emorable (second edition) | **C**opycat                                     |
| **I**magery: gives the mind a picture                          | **R**estrictive: boxes the brand in             |
| **L**egs: gives the brand something to build on                | **A**nnoying                                    |
| **E**motional: makes people feel something                     | **T**ame                                        |
|                                                                | **C**urse of knowledge: speaks only to insiders |
|                                                                | **H**ard to pronounce                           |

Judgment: "Restrictive" is the tension in huntline's own rule. A job-related name is restrictive by design, and the maintainer accepted that trade for findability, as round 5 recorded (S19). "Curse of knowledge" is the strongest warning for any name drawn from developer or Claude Code vocabulary.

### The radio test and equivalents

No primary source for the "radio test" was found in this research, so no claim is made about its origin or its official wording. As a working check, this document defines the equivalent in the rubric (section 8): say the name aloud to someone who has never seen it, and ask them to write it down. If they spell it differently from the real spelling, it fails. Rhythm, spelling and length are covered by the same check and by the SCRATCH list above.

## 6. Checks before adopting a name

Not legal advice. A serious clearance needs a trademark attorney.

### Cross-language meanings

The audience includes Latin America, so check English, Spanish and Portuguese, in that order of priority. One source lists ignoring global discrepancies, names with embarrassing meanings in other languages, as a named mistake, and recommends a word-meanings check covering idioms, slang and cultural implications (S13). Another recommends testing with several native speakers in each target country instead of a few friends, and asking whether the name reminds them of a brand in their country (S12). In the EU, a sign descriptive in any one official language can be refused (S14).

Method for a solo maintainer, Judgment:

1. Look the string up as a word, a prefix and a suffix in a Spanish and a Portuguese dictionary.
2. Say it aloud as a Spanish speaker and as a Portuguese speaker would read it and see whether it forms a different word.
3. Search the name plus "slang" or "significado" for the two languages.
4. Ask one native speaker of each language for a first reaction, before any explanation.

### Trademark search in software classes

| Item                | What the sources say                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Source   |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------- |
| Classification      | The Nice Classification is the international classification of goods and services used to register marks, established by the Nice Agreement in 1957                                                                    | S16      |
| Software classes    | Per a USPTO notice and secondary guides, downloadable software falls in class 9 and software provided as a service in class 42; software companies often file in both                                                  | S17      |
| USPTO               | The USPTO's Trademark Search system is the free database; the page does not say how complete results are                                                                                                               | S15      |
| EUIPO and worldwide | TMview is EUIPO's free search across participating offices, and shows only marks held by participating offices and only what the search wording matched; a search in any database cannot replace a professional search | S18      |
| What to look for    | Same or similar names, spellings and sounds, in classes 9 and 42 and in the job-service classes near them                                                                                                              | Judgment |

Also run the existing automated filter used in the earlier rounds: npm, GitHub slug, `.dev` and `.com`, and a web search for a software product of the same name (S19). A web search is not a trademark search.

### Domain and handle checks

- Domains: a short name registers more easily, and there are about 200 million registered URLs, so assume the first choice is taken (S13).
- Handles: the source for this document does not address social handles (S13). Check GitHub, npm and the one or two social networks the maintainer will actually use, by hand, on the day of the pick.

## 7. Naming brief for huntline

Derived from [brief.md](brief.md), [PRODUCT.md](../../PRODUCT.md), the glossary and the maintainer's rules from rounds 1 to 5 (S19).

**What it is.** A local-first job-search system for Claude Code, a plugin plus a CLI. It sweeps Sources, builds CVs only from Fact bank facts, assists form filling in the human's browser, and syncs replies. You press Submit.

**Who it is for.** A job seeker already working in Claude Code who wants help with repetitive work without handing over control.

**Must**

- ONE word. Not two words stuck together. A small friendly ending that carries no meaning of its own counts as one word.
- English. Not Spanish. Spanish readers should still find it harmless (section 6).
- Memorable and friendly, in the spirit of "Jobbie". Jobbie itself and its close variants are taken, and Jobbie has an auto-apply tone.
- Clearly job-related, so a stranger finds it: someone reading the name cold thinks of jobs, work, hiring, careers or applying.
- Reads well as `/<name>:sweep`, typed in lowercase, with no dash.
- Free on npm and as a GitHub slug.

**Must not**

- Hunting or weapon imagery, including chase and catch verbs.
- Claude orange or clay, sparks; LinkedIn blue; purple.
- "AI magic", auto-apply tone, or any hint that the tool applies for the user.
- A promise of speed, hit rate or outcome.
- A compound of two real words.

**Feeling.** Precise, calm, trustworthy, with warmth. The human stays in control. The line or trail is the governing metaphor, though the maintainer's later rounds put warmth and the job cue ahead of it.

**What the research adds.** Pick a territory first (below). Name length near the SEEK and Jobbie scale. Test by saying and typing, not by looking (section 8). Leave the trademark and language checks for the final three.

## 8. Rubric update

The eight axes and their 0 to 10 scale stay, for a total out of 80: Sound, Registry collision, Brand collision, Brief fit, Headroom, Warmth, Overpromise risk, Findability (S19). Two tests from section 3 are added as gates run before scoring, so they do not change the total.

**Memorability test (gate).** Show the name once, hide it, and ask four people for it again after one other task. Pass if at least three recall it exactly. Backed by the SMILE "Memorable" and SCRATCH "Tame" and "Copycat" items (S6). A fail caps Sound at 5.

**Pronunciation and spelling test (gate).** Say the name aloud, without showing it, to four people and ask them to type it. Pass if at least three type the real spelling and nobody asks how to say it. Backed by SCRATCH "Spelling-challenged" and "Hard to pronounce" (S6) and the finding that fluently pronounced names are processed more favourably (S10). A fail caps Sound at 5. This is also the radio-test equivalent noted in section 3.

**Two fixed checks, pass or fail, before any score**:

- **The `:sweep` test.** Type `/<name>:sweep` and read it aloud. It must read as a name, not as a phrase.
- **The one-word and job-cue test.** One word, and a stranger who has not been told what it does names a job-related meaning.

| D. The craft of getting hired           | Brief fit, Overpromise risk                  | Trades words can imply the tool is the student or the worker                                |
| E. A verb you say to a friend           | Registry collision, Overpromise risk         | Plain verbs are mostly taken; verbs near "hired" imply an outcome                           |
| F. A coined word from a job root        | Sound, Brand collision, Findability          | Coined words rise or fall on whether they sound natural and avoid ATS-style near-names      |
| G. A place you wait                     | Findability, Warmth                          | A calm image with a weak job link                                                           |
| H. Standing behind your word            | Findability, Brief fit, Overpromise risk     | The strongest match to the promise, but abstract and potentially legalistic                 |

Headroom stays at its low weight in practice: the maintainer traded it for findability in round 5 (S19).
